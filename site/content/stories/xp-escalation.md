<p class="sec-label">// 01 the situation</p>
<h2>The top support tier for Windows XP</h2>
<p class="lead">Early in my career, on a Microsoft support contract through Convergys, I worked at the top support tier for Windows XP: the Level 3, R&amp;D desk.</p>
<p>The role started with a month of Microsoft training on troubleshooting the OS, and within a few months I had become the go-to: the cases Level 1 and Level 2 couldn't solve came to me.</p>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-label">// 02 what I did</p>
<h2>Find the cause, then write it down</h2>
<ol class="parts">
<li>
<div class="part-h">
<span class="n">1</span><h3>Root-cause diagnosis</h3>
</div>
<p>The work was root-cause diagnosis. I traced what had actually changed on a machine, using Event Viewer and Microsoft's knowledge base, both the public articles and the internal ones, to pin down corrupted registry entries, bad drivers, and damaged system files.</p>
</li>
<li>
<div class="part-h">
<span class="n">2</span><h3>Write it up for the next person</h3>
</div>
<p>I also became a frequent contributor to that knowledge base myself, writing my own KB articles on registry and system fixes so the next person could solve it faster.</p>
</li>
</ol>
</section>
<section>
<p class="sec-label">// 03 what I take from it</p>
<h2>The person hard problems escalate to</h2>
<p class="lead">It's the earliest sustained example of a pattern that has followed me since: being the person hard problems escalate to, and solving them by finding the cause instead of guessing at it.</p>
<p>The same instinct turns up later in reading firewall logs and tuning monitoring.</p>
</section>
